## Part A — journal-style review

You are a referee for a mathematical-imaging journal (JMIV class), reviewing the attached
manuscript, *Spatial scale space from hemigroup axioms: characterization of the kernels on the
line*. Write a detailed referee report. Be specific, cite pages of the PDF, and separate what
is wrong from what is merely improvable.

1. **Overall assessment and recommendation** (accept, minor revision, major revision, reject),
   with the reasons in a paragraph.

2. **The headline claims against what is proved.** The abstract, the contributions list in
   section 1, and the concluding section make claims; check each against the theorems and
   proofs that are supposed to carry it. Say where a claim is stronger than its result, where
   a qualification made in the body is missing from the headline, and where the scope
   statements ("outside this paper") leave a reader with a wrong impression.

3. **Mathematical correctness.** Read the statements and proofs. Report every gap, error, or
   unjustified step you find, with the page. Pay particular attention to
   - the parts the article says are not machine-checked (section 1.1 and the point of claim:
     the construction in Proposition 3.5, and the identification of the Matérn member's
     kernel as a closed form), where the printed argument is all there is;
   - the cited facts of Table 1 and whether each is used within its stated hypotheses;
   - the hypothesis checks where a classical result is applied (Propositions 7.8 and 7.9);
   - Lemma 4.2 (nonvanishing), Theorem 5.2 (the null-array limit and the truncation
     inequality), Lemma 6.4 and Proposition 6.6 (the rigidity of the action and the gauge),
     and Lemma 7.1 (the self-decomposability lemma), which carry the characterization.

4. **The trust base.** Section 1.1 and Table 1 state what the machine-checked development
   assumes. Is the account clear, complete and honest as a reader would judge it? Is anything
   claimed as verified that the text elsewhere qualifies?

5. **Novelty and relation to prior work.** The article positions itself against Pauwels, Van
   Gool, Fiddelaers and Moons (1995) and the Gaussian axiomatics (Iijima, Koenderink,
   Lindeberg, Florack), and against the probability literature on self-decomposable laws
   (Sato, Steutel and van Harn). Is the positioning accurate? Is anything claimed as new that
   is known, or attributed wrongly? Is relevant work missing?

6. **Exposition and structure.** Where does the argument become hard to follow, where is a
   definition used before it is given, where is a forward reference load-bearing, and which
   remarks or paragraphs could be cut without loss?

7. **A numbered list of required changes** (what must change for acceptance) and a separate
   **numbered list of suggestions** (what would improve the paper). Each item with a page
   reference and one sentence of reason.

Do not summarize the paper back to the author beyond what the assessment needs. Do not
soften findings; a wrong claim should be called wrong. If you are unsure whether something is
an error, say so and say what would settle it.

## Part B — presentation review

Read the attached manuscript as an experienced reader of mathematical-imaging papers, not as
a referee of its mathematics. Report on presentation only:

- the abstract and introduction: does a reader of the field know after two pages what the
  paper proves, why it matters, and what it does not claim?
- the register: sentences that are hard to parse, metaphors or coined terms that a reader
  would not follow, places where the text announces rather than states, and any passage that
  reads as written for the author's own project rather than for the field;
- the figures and tables: is each one earning its place, and is anything missing a figure;
- the notation: collisions, symbols used before they are introduced, and the notation
  table's usefulness;
- the section structure: does each section open with what it is for, and do the section
  ends land on content rather than on a flourish.

Give located items (page and quoted phrase) and, where you can, the plainer alternative. Keep
the list short and ranked; twenty located items are worth more than a hundred.
